Mixed reality for the ToteASRS pick workflow

What could fulfillment-center work look like five years from now? AugmenTote explores mixed reality as a flexible layer over the ToteASRS pick workflow—bringing spatial guidance, contextual information, responsive help, and more engaging moments directly into the associate's field of view.

CONTEXT

Work at Amazon Robotics

APPLICATION

ToteASRS Pick workflow across fulfillment centers

SOFTWARE + HARDWARE

Unity3D, C#, Magic Leap 2, mixed-reality prototyping

Background

Why mixed reality?

Extended reality blends the physical and digital. For this project, mixed reality is the useful middle ground: virtual information can react to and sit within the real work environment instead of replacing it.

Flexible Infrastructure

Software can be updated more easily than hardware. The same glasses can support many situations across fulfillment centers and help bridge locations running different generations of physical equipment.

Smart Learning + Support

Personalized guidance, feedback, training, knowledge sharing, and clearer access to the information required to complete a task.

Connected Culture

More opportunities for social interaction, entertainment, and healthier work practices within the scope of the role.

Case study

ToteASRS + the Pick workflow

ToteASRS picking supports low-volume and sub-same-day delivery, but the workflow can involve 8–12 hour shifts of repetitive and physically demanding work. AugmenTote asks where mixed reality can reduce ambiguity and repetitive searching without adding another screen to manage.

Prototype

Unity + C# + Magic Leap 2

The prototype places graphics directly over the station and rebin wall so information appears in the spatial context where it is needed. Early experiments explored item information, scanned-state feedback, directional indicators, help controls, and visual cues attached to physical totes and slots.

User Experience Requirement #1

INCREASED EFFICIENCY

Put the right information where the work happens

The current interface surfaces product name, ASIN, product image, associate information, and issue-reporting controls. The mixed-reality proposal moves the most useful pieces of that information into the worker's view so less attention has to travel back and forth between the station and a separate screen.

Associates described checking the barcode first, then using product description details such as material or color when an item is hard to identify.

3D item guidance

Product name and ASIN can accompany a spatial representation of the item. A future goal is to use computer vision to help with the seek process and make the desired item easier to locate within a tote.

Rebin wall guides + slot transition indicators

The proposal adds spatial cues to the rebin wall: indicators can distinguish the current slot from ongoing orders, communicate how many items remain in an order, and make tote or slot transitions harder to miss. The goal is to reduce avoidable running back and forth caused by transitions that are difficult to predict or easy to overlook.

  • Current slot: highlighted directly in space.
  • Ongoing order: stays visible without appearing active.
  • Order quantity: visual state changes with the number of items.
  • Transition: directional and blinking cues signal the next action.

User Experience Requirement #2

SMART LEARNING + SUPPORT

Help without leaving the task

A wrist-accessible help menu can expose issue reporting, manager contact, guided help, breaks, and optional game modes. Instead of requiring the associate to translate a problem into the correct menu path, the system can respond to a spoken description of the issue.

PeccAI is proposed as a responsive helper: it detects that the associate may be struggling, asks them to describe the problem, and maps the response to the appropriate help state.

The prototype listens for speech, converts audio to text, classifies the issue, then displays the matching support flow. Example categories include missing or damaged items, scanning problems, difficulty grabbing an item, a requested break, or the need to contact a manager.

Examples in the study include an associate saying they cannot find an item, that a tote appears empty, or that an item is damaged or will not scan.

User Experience Requirement #3

WELLNESS + WORK SATISFACTION

Can 8–12 hours of picking be made less boring?

Existing fulfillment-center games can sit in the background without meaningfully engaging the associate. The proposal reframes gamification so the challenge is attached to the actual pick: some picks stay regular while others become specific challenges with direct rewards or competitive effects.

Challenge families

  • Speed: timed picks that can boost points.
  • Placement + type: stars, gems, or targets that reward particular item types, quantities, or orientations.
  • Wellness: non-dominant-hand picks, hydration reminders, and stretch suggestions.
  • Competitive: shields, fog, fire, and bombs that affect an opponent's temporary game state.

Wellness should not depend on the game

The research also points to direct wellness features: music, visible progress, and scheduled stretch or rest prompts. Associates specifically described pain building through long periods of standing and said short, recurring stretch breaks could help their workflow.

Wrap up

Barriers to implementation

Current glasses still face practical constraints: an eight-hour battery target is difficult, more capable headsets can be heavy or bulky, and there are unresolved questions around shared devices versus issuing a pair to every associate. The presentation frames these as moving constraints as wearable technology develops.

What the prototype can do today

Even before full deployment, the mixed-reality prototype is useful for testing UX proposals without fully coding or fabricating every system, moving into user testing earlier, sharing prototype states between locations, and letting employees experience proposed processes directly in AR.
